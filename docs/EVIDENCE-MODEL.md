# Evidence Model

## Purpose

The evidence model defines how observations about a learner may support, weaken, or leave unresolved a claim about developing ability or career fit.

## Evidence categories

Possible categories include:

### Interest evidence

Examples:

* recurring voluntary questions;
* repeated exploration;
* sustained attention to a topic;
* self-initiated learning.

Interest should not be treated as proof of ability.

### Performance evidence

Examples:

* assignments;
* projects;
* demonstrations;
* simulations;
* examinations;
* real-world tasks.

### Learning-velocity evidence

Examples:

* improvement after instruction;
* responsiveness to feedback;
* number of attempts required;
* transfer to new situations.

### Behavioral evidence

Examples:

* persistence;
* collaboration;
* response to ambiguity;
* response to pressure;
* planning;
* self-correction.

Behavior should always be interpreted in context.

### Human expert evidence

Examples:

* teacher evaluation;
* mentor observation;
* professional critique;
* supervisor feedback.

Expert status should not make an observation automatically infallible.

### Artifact evidence

Examples:

* writing;
* code;
* designs;
* portfolios;
* performances;
* physical products;
* recorded demonstrations.

### Self-report evidence

Examples:

* motivation;
* perceived confidence;
* preferred environment;
* lifestyle preferences;
* career aspirations.

Self-report is meaningful evidence but not identical to demonstrated performance.

## Evidence provenance

Every important evidence record should ideally answer:

* Who or what produced the evidence?
* When?
* Under what conditions?
* Based on which task or artifact?
* Using which rubric or criteria?
* How reliable is the observation?
* What are its limitations?

## Claim strength

A claim should never be stronger than the evidence supporting it.

Possible states may eventually include:

* observed;
* weakly supported;
* moderately supported;
* strongly supported;
* contradicted;
* disputed;
* not yet assessed.

The exact vocabulary remains open for research.

## Contradictory evidence

Contradictions should not be silently averaged away.

The system should preserve:

* conflicting observations;
* evaluator disagreement;
* contextual differences;
* temporal changes.

Contradiction may itself be useful evidence.

## Missing evidence

Missing evidence must remain distinguishable from negative evidence.

> **Not observed ≠ unable.**

This principle is especially important when learners have unequal access to opportunities.
