# Contributing to Career Evidence Loop

Career Evidence Loop is an early-stage, interdisciplinary research project.

You do not need to be a researcher, agree with the current framework, or arrive with a complete solution to contribute.

A useful contribution might be a question, a counterexample, a research paper, a practitioner perspective, a correction, a failure case, or a proposal that helps the project ask a better question.

## Choose a way to contribute

Different contributions require different levels of effort.

### Share a question, critique, or perspective

If something seems unclear, too simple, unsupported, or wrong, start a discussion.

Useful contributions include:

- a question the current framework does not answer;
- a counterexample;
- a missing perspective;
- a practical concern from your field;
- an existing system or research area the project should examine;
- a reason an assumption may fail in practice.

You do not need to propose a solution.

A well-framed problem can be a valuable contribution on its own.

### Point to relevant research

If you know a paper, framework, dataset, review, or research tradition that may help, share it.

Please explain briefly why it is relevant rather than posting a citation alone.

For deeper research contributions, see [Research contributions](#research-contributions).

### Make a small correction

Documentation fixes, terminology improvements, broken links, unclear wording, and corrections to inaccurate claims can usually go directly through a focused pull request.

### Develop a larger proposal

For changes to the evidence model, career-hypothesis model, conceptual architecture, ethics framework, schemas, or other substantial parts of the project, open an issue or discussion first.

The proposal does not need to be complete.

Start with the problem you think needs attention.

### Build or test something

Prototype ideas, schemas, examples, and small experiments are welcome when they help test a specific assumption or research question.

Please make clear what the work is intended to test and what conclusions it cannot support.

---

## What the project currently needs

The project is still in an early research phase.

Contributions are especially useful when they help:

- sharpen the problem definition;
- identify relevant existing research or systems;
- challenge assumptions;
- distinguish concepts that may have been combined too quickly;
- improve how evidence and uncertainty are represented;
- identify ethical, safety, fairness, or privacy risks;
- understand where human expertise should enter the loop;
- identify failure cases or counterexamples;
- turn broad ideas into questions that could eventually be tested;
- improve terminology or documentation.

Relevant perspectives may come from education, learning sciences, career development, psychology, assessment, AI, HCI, software engineering, design, or domain practice.

Other perspectives are welcome when they help illuminate the problem.

---

## Principles to preserve

Contributions should remain consistent with a few core boundaries:

1. Separate observation from inference.
2. Do not present career hypotheses as deterministic conclusions.
3. Do not treat missing evidence as evidence of low potential.
4. Preserve learner agency.
5. Make uncertainty visible where it matters.
6. Prefer verifiable sources for empirical claims.
7. Do not include identifiable learner data without appropriate consent and governance.
8. Do not infer high-stakes professional competence from AI interaction alone.

These principles are not intended to prevent criticism of the framework.

If you think one of them is incomplete or should be revised, explain why and what evidence or reasoning supports the challenge.

## Research contributions

When adding research, provide enough information for another contributor to find and evaluate the source.

Where possible, include:

- author(s);
- year;
- title;
- publication or venue;
- DOI or stable URL;
- the specific idea relevant to this project;
- important limitations or cautions.

Avoid adding a source only because its title appears relevant.

Please read enough of the source to distinguish what it actually supports from what you infer from it.

A useful research contribution should make clear:

**Source claim → interpretation → possible CEL relevance → limitations or open questions**

### Literature notes

Working research notes belong in:

`research/literature-notes/`

A literature note should distinguish:

- what the source reports;
- your interpretation;
- how it may relate to Career Evidence Loop;
- important limitations;
- questions that remain unresolved.

A literature note does not need to prove that a source supports the project.

Evidence that challenges the project is equally relevant.

---

## Proposing conceptual or architectural changes

For substantial changes, start from the problem rather than the solution.

Explain, where relevant:

- what problem you are trying to solve;
- what current assumption or model you are challenging;
- what change you propose;
- the evidence or reasoning behind it;
- possible risks or trade-offs;
- what remains uncertain;
- what would need to be tested.

Do not feel obligated to fill every category if it does not apply.

An incomplete proposal with a clear problem can be more useful than a polished solution built on an unclear assumption.

---

## Examples and scenarios

Examples can help expose hidden assumptions and failure cases.

They should be:

- fictional or appropriately anonymized;
- understandable without unnecessary specialist knowledge;
- explicit about what is observed versus inferred;
- cautious about career recommendations;
- useful for examining a specific question or part of the framework.

Real learner data should not be included without appropriate consent and governance.

---

## AI-assisted contributions

AI tools may assist with drafting, coding, summarization, research exploration, or critique.

Contributors remain responsible for:

- factual accuracy;
- source verification;
- appropriate attribution;
- checking that citations exist and support the claim;
- protecting private information;
- distinguishing evidence from interpretation;
- ensuring that the final contribution reflects their actual reasoning.

Do not rely on AI-generated references without verifying the original source.

If AI materially shaped a research synthesis or analysis, consider noting that in the pull request when the information would help reviewers understand how the work was produced.

## How to participate

### Discussion first

Use GitHub Discussions when you want to:

- ask a question;
- challenge an assumption;
- share a perspective or counterexample;
- suggest research worth examining;
- explore an idea before defining a concrete change.

You do not need a finished proposal.

### Open an issue

Use an issue when there is a more bounded problem, research task, documentation gap, or proposed change that could lead to concrete work.

For larger conceptual changes, discussing the problem before implementation can help avoid unnecessary work.

### Open a pull request

A focused pull request is appropriate for:

- documentation improvements;
- corrections;
- literature notes;
- examples;
- agreed conceptual changes;
- schemas or prototypes with a clear purpose.

Explain what changed, why it matters, and any important assumptions or limitations.

---

## Review mindset

The goal is not to make the framework look complete or correct.

The goal is to make it more accurate, testable, and useful over time.

Good contributions may:

- support an idea;
- narrow a claim;
- reveal missing evidence;
- show that two concepts should be separated;
- identify a risk;
- replace an assumption;
- show that part of the framework is unnecessary.

Agreement is not the measure of a useful contribution.

Specific reasoning and evidence matter more.

---

## Not sure where to start?

You can begin with one question:

> **What does this project seem to be assuming that may not be true?**

Or choose one part of the repository that overlaps with something you know and point out what looks missing, unclear, or worth testing.

A small, specific contribution is enough to start.
